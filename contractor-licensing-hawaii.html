Hawaii Law

What HRS 444-2 Means for Homeowners

Hawaii's contractor licensing law protects you. Here is when you need a licensed contractor, what the $1,000 exemption actually means, and how to verify a license before hiring.

The Law: HRS 444-2

Hawaii Revised Statutes Chapter 444 regulates contractor licensing. The key provision homeowners encounter is Section 444-2, which defines exemptions from the licensing requirement:

"Any project or operation for which the aggregate contract price for labor, materials, taxes, and all other items is not more than $1,000. This exemption shall not apply in any case where a building permit is required regardless of the aggregate contract price, nor where the undertaking is only a part of a larger or major project or operation, whether undertaken by the same or a different contractor or in which a division of the project or operation is made in contracts of amounts not more than $1,000 for the purpose of evading this chapter or otherwise." -- HRS 444-2(4)

What This Means in Practice

The $1,000 Exemption

Work under $1,000 total (labor + materials + tax) does not require a licensed contractor. This covers genuinely small jobs: a minor plumbing fix, patching a small section of drywall, or replacing a few outlet covers.

Building Permit Overrides the Dollar Amount

If the work requires a building permit, you need a licensed contractor regardless of cost. Even a $500 job needs a licensed contractor if a permit is required. In Hawaii, permits are required for:

  • Electrical work beyond simple fixture replacements
  • Plumbing work involving supply or drainage lines
  • Structural changes (removing walls, adding rooms)
  • Roofing replacements
  • HVAC installation
  • Water heater installation or replacement
  • Solar panel installation
  • Any new construction or additions

You Cannot Split a Job to Avoid the Law

The law explicitly prohibits dividing a project into multiple contracts under $1,000 to evade licensing requirements. A $3,000 bathroom remodel cannot be split into four $750 contracts. Courts look at the total scope of the project, not how the contracts are structured.

Why this matters: If an unlicensed person does work that requires a license and something goes wrong, your homeowner's insurance may deny the claim. You may also be unable to recover damages in court because the contract was illegal.

How to Verify a Contractor's License

Before hiring any contractor in Hawaii, verify their license through the Department of Commerce and Consumer Affairs (DCCA):

  1. Go to the DCCA Professional and Vocational Licensing portal at pvl.ehawaii.gov
  2. Search by the contractor's name or license number
  3. Verify the license is current (not expired or inactive)
  4. Check the license classification matches the work being done (electrical contractors need a C-13 specialty license)
  5. Confirm there are no disciplinary actions or complaints
License Classifications

A -- General Engineering Contractor
B -- General Building Contractor
C -- Specialty Contractor (includes C-13 Electrical, C-37 Plumbing, C-42 Roofing, and others)

An electrician must hold a C-13 license. A general contractor (B license) cannot perform electrical work without a C-13 subcontractor.

What Happens When Someone Works Without a License

Performing contractor work without a license in Hawaii is a misdemeanor offense. Penalties include:

  • Fines up to $2,000 per violation
  • Possible imprisonment up to one year
  • The unlicensed person cannot sue to collect payment for the work
  • The homeowner may be able to recover all payments made for unlicensed work

Additionally, the DCCA Regulated Industries Complaints Office (RICO) investigates complaints about unlicensed contracting. You can file a complaint at cca.hawaii.gov/rico or call (808) 586-2643.

Protecting Yourself as a Homeowner

  • Always verify the license before signing a contract or making any payment
  • Get everything in writing -- scope of work, materials, timeline, total cost, payment schedule
  • Confirm insurance -- ask for a certificate of general liability insurance and workers' compensation insurance
  • Pull the permits yourself or verify they were pulled -- you can check permit status at the Department of Planning and Permitting
  • Never pay the full amount upfront -- a reasonable structure is 10-30% deposit, progress payments, and final payment upon completion and inspection
  • Get at least three written estimates for any job over $1,000
